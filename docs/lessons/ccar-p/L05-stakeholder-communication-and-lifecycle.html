Time: 90 min plus the paper exercise · Practice: Lesson 5 practice set Colleague note: a colleague who passed (944) said about half the exam is standard system design with Claude wording. So real engagement experience (discovery, RACI, rollout, change management) transfers directly. This lesson adds the Claude-specific twists.

6.0 The big idea

Most of this domain is ordinary good consulting with a Claude twist. Find out what people really need, write down why you chose what you chose, bring bad news early with numbers and options, and hand over something the next team can run.

6.1 Structured discovery and requirements

Discovery comes before architecture. Work through it in this order:

StepAskClaude-specific twist
ProblemWhat decision or task is slow, costly or error-prone today?Is an LLM even the right tool, or would a rule or a search box do?
Users and stakeholdersWho uses it, who owns it, who signs off, who can say no (security, legal, compliance)?Who reviews AI output, and who is accountable for it?
Success measuresWhat number must move, and from what baseline?Set eval metrics now: accuracy, latency, cost, safety
ConstraintsBudget, latency, data sensitivity, regulation, existing systemsData residency, retention, which data may reach the model
DataWhat data exists, where, how clean, who owns it?Is there a labeled set to evaluate against? If not, build one
Risk toleranceWhat's the cost of a wrong answer?Drives human-in-the-loop design (L04)
  • A RACI (responsible, accountable, consulted, informed) for each decision stops "who approves the prompt change?" from becoming an incident.
  • Capture non-goals. What the system won't do is as important as what it will.

6.2 Communicating decisions and trade-offs: the ADR

An architecture decision record (ADR) is a short document per significant decision:

SectionContents
Title and status"ADR-004: Use retrieval, not fine-tuning, for policy answers" · Proposed / Accepted / Superseded
ContextThe problem and the constraints that force a choice
Options consideredAt least two real alternatives, each with pros and cons
DecisionWhat you chose
ConsequencesWhat gets better, what gets worse, what you'll monitor, when you'll revisit
  • A decision record without alternatives is a press release. The trade-offs are the point.
  • Tailor the message, not the facts. Executives get outcome, cost and risk; engineers get the design; compliance gets data flows and controls. Same numbers for everyone.
  • Present honest, segmented numbers. "94% overall, 71% on handwritten forms" beats a single flattering average. Say how you measured it.

6.3 Feedback loops and expectations (including SLAs)

  • Set expectations about non-determinism early. Stakeholders used to deterministic software need to hear that an AI system is measured on a rate across a test set, not on one demo.
  • Agree SLAs in terms you can measure:
SLA itemExample target (set per project)Measured by
AvailabilityAgreed uptimeMonitoring
Latencyp95 response timeTraces
QualityAccuracy on the eval set, per segmentRegression suite
EscalationShare of cases routed to a human, and human turnaroundQueue metrics
CostCost per task or per month ceilingUsage reports
  • Include a quality SLA, not just uptime. A system that is up and wrong is failing.
  • Feedback loops: capture user ratings and reviewer corrections, turn them into new eval cases, and report back what changed. Stakeholders who see their feedback used keep giving it.

6.4 Documentation and implementation guidance

  • Architecture docs: context diagram, data flow (what data goes to the model and what comes back), components, the ADR log.
  • Implementation guidance for the build team: prompt and template locations, tool contracts, eval commands, how to add a test case, and how to roll back.
  • Runbooks for operations: known failure modes and their first diagnostic step.
  • A handoff with diagrams but no "how to change it safely" guidance is incomplete.

6.5 Lifecycle support

An AI system is never "done". Each phase below has a job for the architect and a clear sign it's time to move on:

PhaseArchitect's jobExit criterion
DiscoveryProblem, constraints, success measures, riskSigned-off requirements and metrics
DesignArchitecture, ADRs, eval planDesign reviewed by security and compliance
Build and handoffGuidance, eval suite, runbooksBuild team can change and test it without you
MonitoringDashboards on quality, latency, cost, escalationsAlerts are tied to SLAs
IterationFeedback into evals, A/B tests, prompt and model updatesChanges pass the regression suite first
  • Change management is part of the design: train reviewers, roll out in stages (pilot group, then wider), and keep the old process as a fallback until the new one meets its SLAs.
  • Model upgrades are a lifecycle event. Run the eval suite on the new model before switching.

Worked example

A logistics firm's operations director asks for "an AI that answers carrier questions." Discovery shows the real pain is 40 minutes per email looking up contract terms, the contracts contain confidential rates, and legal must approve anything sent externally. Response: write down the problem, the baseline and the non-goals (no automatic replies to carriers). Record ADR-001: retrieval over the contract library, with drafts reviewed by staff. Agree SLAs for p95 latency, accuracy on a labeled set of 200 past questions, and review turnaround. Pilot with one team, feed corrections back into the eval set, and report segmented results monthly.

Traps

  • Jumping to architecture before success measures are agreed.
  • An ADR listing only the chosen option.
  • An SLA with uptime but no quality measure.
  • One flattering average instead of results by segment.
  • Treating handoff as "send the diagram."

Try it (30 min): paper exercise

Brief: a property-management company wants Claude to summarize tenant maintenance requests and suggest a priority for dispatchers. Requests contain tenants' names and addresses; dispatchers make the final call; leadership wants "faster repairs." Write a one-page ADR for one decision (for example, which model tier, or batch vs real time) with at least two options and their consequences. Then write an SLA table with five rows: availability, latency, quality, escalation and cost, each with how you'd measure it.
