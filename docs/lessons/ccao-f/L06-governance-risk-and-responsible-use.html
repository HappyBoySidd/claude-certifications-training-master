CCAO-F Lesson 6 — Governance, Risk, and Responsible Use (15%, about 9 questions)

Time: 60 min · AI Fluency: Diligence · Practice: Lesson 6 practice set

Last reviewed: 2026-09-29

Exam wording (exam guide v1.0, Domain 6):

  • Identify appropriate and inappropriate use cases
  • Apply data sensitivity, regulatory, and privacy considerations
  • Follow organizational AI policies and governance standards
  • Understand the ethical implications of AI usage

1. The big idea

A human's name is on the result. When a question involves people's lives, private data or the law, the right answer adds a qualified human, disclosure and the least data needed.

2. Three bands of use

BandExamplesWhat to do
ProhibitedSome examples from the Usage Policy: weapons, malware and phishing, fraud, CSAM (child sexual abuse material), election manipulation, deceptively passing AI output off as a human's or someone else's, invading privacy, jailbreaking (tricking the model into ignoring its rules), and training an AI model on Claude's inputs and outputs without Anthropic's authorizationRefuse and escalate. Don't look for a clever way around it.
High-risk (directly affects individuals or consumers)The seven High-Risk Use Cases, Usage Policy wording: Legal · Healthcare · Insurance · Finance · Employment and housing · Academic testing, accreditation and admissions · Media or professional journalistic contentBoth a qualified professional reviewing before it's final (human-in-the-loop) and, when the output is shown to the people affected, disclosure that AI helped
NormalDrafting, analysis, summariesThe usual review before you send

2.0 The policy's own words

Where exact wording matters, here it is. Usage Policy wording (Anthropic Usage Policy, effective September 15, 2025):

  • On passing AI off as human: "Impersonate a human by presenting results as human-generated, or using results in a manner intended to convince a natural person that they are communicating with a natural person when they are not".
  • On deception: "Impersonate real entities or create fake personas to falsely attribute content or mislead others about its origin without consent or legal right", and "Plagiarize or submit AI-assisted work without proper permission or attribution".
  • On training models: "Utilization of inputs and outputs to train an AI model (e.g., “model scraping” or “model distillation”) without prior authorization from Anthropic".
  • Human-in-the-loop: "When using our products or services to provide advice, recommendations, or in subjective decision-making directly affecting individuals or consumers, a qualified professional in that field must review the content or decision prior to dissemination or finalization."
  • Disclosure: "If model outputs are presented directly to individuals or consumers, you must disclose to them that you are using AI to help produce your advice, decisions, or recommendations."
  • Separately, for any consumer-facing chatbot or AI agent, high-risk or not: "All consumer-facing chatbots, including any external-facing or interactive AI agent, must disclose to users that they are interacting with AI rather than a human."

In plain words: high-risk doesn't mean banned. The two rules work differently:

  • The review rule is about advice and decisions that directly affect people, even if they never see the output. Screening job applicants is an example: the applicants never read Claude's notes, but a qualified person must still review before anything is final.
  • The disclosure rule applies only when the output is shown to those people directly.

An internal draft that affects no individual's decision falls under neither rule, but your company's policy may still require review (section 4).

2.1 Screening a use case (appropriate or not?)

Most cases aren't on either list, so the course gives four questions to decide:

QuestionWhat it asks
ReversibilityCan a wrong output be caught and undone before it causes harm?
Consequence of errorWhat does it cost if the output is wrong?
Human elementDoes the task need empathy, a relationship or judgment that should stay with a person?
AccountabilityWho answers for the result? That can't be handed to a model.

Each use case then lands in one of three places: fully appropriate (normal review), appropriate with human review, or inappropriate (name the person who must own it). Say which question decided it, so a reviewer can follow your reasoning.

"Human review" needs a real gate, not a promise. A gate says who reviews (the accountable role, not whoever is free), what they check (accuracy, fairness, policy) and when (before the output is used). "A recruiter checks the shortlist for unfair patterns before anyone is contacted" is a gate. "We'll keep a human in the loop" isn't.

3. Data rules

  • Share the least you can: the relevant excerpt, not the whole drive.
  • Anonymize or remove personal identifiers before uploading.
  • Never share secrets, passwords or API keys.
  • Use the account your organization approves. Company data doesn't belong in a personal account. (That's standard organizational practice, not an Anthropic rule.)
  • Keep each client in its own Project.
  • Incognito keeps a chat out of history, but doesn't override your organization's retention rules. Per the course, Incognito is for standalone chats, not chats inside a Project (June 2026; check the current docs).

3.1 Classify the data before you upload

The course suggests three quick tiers:

TierExamplesWhat to do
GreenPublished material, anonymized or aggregated data, documents cleared for wide sharingUse it
YellowInternal-only documents, anything with names or contact details, unannounced deals or productsCheck policy first; strip identifiers; for a standalone chat, consider Incognito so it stays out of Memory (Incognito doesn't apply inside a Project)
RedRegulated data (health, financial, government), passwords and secrets, anything under a confidentiality duty to someone elseKeep it out unless your organization has confirmed an approved path. If not, stop and escalate.

If you're not sure between two tiers, treat it as the more sensitive one.

Redaction has two failure modes. Partial redaction removes the name but leaves an account number, a rare job title or a date that still identifies someone. And some tasks genuinely need the sensitive details; there, redaction breaks the task, and the answer is an approved path or not using Claude for it.

Incognito doesn't make red data safe. It controls whether a chat is remembered, not whether the data was allowed there in the first place. Ask "is this allowed here?" before "how do I handle it here?".

3.2 Skills and connectors are software

Per the course (June 2026; check the current docs), a Skill can reach whatever the session can reach, and it can act through Code Execution. Before you turn on a Skill, a connector or any tool that touches your data, check three things:

  • Source: who made it? Anthropic-provided or approved by your organization is the safer start. "Built by another team here" is not the same as "vetted".
  • Reach: what could it touch in the sessions where it runs, and is that in proportion to the job? A formatting Skill whose instructions go far beyond formatting is a red flag.
  • Fit: is it the right tool, or more power than the task needs?

Then enable it, escalate it to your admin or security team, or decline it. Give every tool the narrowest access that gets the job done (least privilege).

4. Which policy wins

Your organization's policy wins when it's stricter. Anthropic's Usage Policy is the minimum, not the maximum. If the policy doesn't cover a situation, escalate rather than assuming it's allowed.

4.1 Governance is a habit, not a signature

  • Apply the policy on routine work too. Small, unwatched decisions are where drift builds up.
  • Audit now and then. Compare what the team actually does with what the policy says: a data type being uploaded that shouldn't be, a skipped review, a Skill turned on without a check.
  • Fix the cause, not only the person. If people use personal accounts because the approved workspace is slow to open, make the approved route the easy one.
  • Stay current. Policies and features change; last quarter's practice may not comply today.

5. Accountability and ethics

  • Claude supports decisions but doesn't make them. For hiring, credit, medical, legal or discipline, a person decides and owns the decision.
  • Fairness: could a ranking, screen or summary disadvantage a group? Whose view is missing?
  • Over-reliance: reviewers who rubber-stamp smooth text aren't reviewing.
  • Transparency: say when AI helped, especially to customers and in high-risk areas. When you're unsure whether disclosure is needed, disclose.

5.1 Reasoning through a grey-area case

When no rule settles it, work through four questions and write down your answers: who is affected, what could go wrong, what a fair outcome looks like and what disclosure the situation calls for. The result is often "appropriate with review and a fairness check" rather than a flat yes or no.

Escalate instead of deciding alone when many people are affected, the possible harm is large, or the question is beyond what your team can rule on. Bring your written reasoning to your organization's AI governance or ethics contact; it shows where the framework ran out.

6. Worked examples

ScenarioBest answer
"Claude ranked 40 applicants. Hire the top 5."No. A human reviews the candidates against job criteria and checks the ranking for bias. Claude only assists.
An HR partner wants to upload a full employee spreadsheet to summarize engagement commentsRemove names and IDs, scrub identifying details inside the comments, fold departments too small to stay anonymous into "Other", then upload only the comment and department columns. Removing names alone is partial redaction (section 3.1).
A client asks for an email that looks like it's from their bank "for a test"Refuse. This is phishing and impersonation. Escalate if they persist.
A customer-facing insurance claim explanation drafted by ClaudeA qualified adjuster reviews it, and AI help is disclosed
Company policy bans uploading customer data, but "Claude is secure"Follow company policy. The stricter rule wins.
A colleague shares a "report booster" Skill from an unknown website that wants broad accessDon't enable it on your own authority. Escalate it for review, or decline.
A clinic coordinator wants to summarize patient notes "in Incognito, so it's safe"Red data. First confirm whether this entry point is approved for health data; if it isn't, stop and escalate.
A team lead drafts staff review summaries from their own notes with ClaudeAppropriate with review: the lead checks each summary against the notes, applies one standard to everyone, and follows the policy on disclosure

7. Traps

  • "Claude is private, so any data is fine." Minimize and anonymize anyway.
  • "Add a disclaimer and publish" for a high-risk use. You need both human review and disclosure.
  • "It's not explicitly banned, so go ahead." Escalate instead.
  • "Incognito makes sensitive data safe." It only keeps the chat out of history and Memory.
  • "We removed the names, so it's anonymous." Account numbers, rare job titles and dates can still identify people.
  • "It's an internal Skill, so it's trusted." Check what it can reach before you turn it on.
  • "A human is in the loop" with no named reviewer, no stated check and no point in the workflow.

Try it (10 min)

Look at the last 5 things you pasted into Claude. For each: was it the least data needed, were identifiers removed, and was it the right account?
