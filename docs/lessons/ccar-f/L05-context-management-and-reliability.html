Stage 1 — A resumed case remembers the old approval

A support case continues tomorrow. A summary remembers the customer’s goal and yesterday’s approved proposal, but the amount has changed. The application needs continuity without treating remembered state as fresh permission.

Original exam-style practice — choose ONE. Which resumption strategy best preserves useful context while respecting changing external facts?

A. Restore a bounded state packet containing goal, decisions, sources and unresolved work, then refresh volatile facts and revalidate approvals before effects.

B. Paste the entire transcript and assume every old fact remains current.

C. Start from nothing and discard the case identifier.

What is this asking? (optional, after reading the choices)

Identify the requirement that the current application fails to meet. Compare the proposed mechanisms against that requirement. Explain unfamiliar terms using the context above, then record a choice and your reason before opening the repair.

Reveal the repair and replay evidence

Choice A. A summary is a handover note, not a live bank record. A retains useful work and rechecks time-sensitive facts. B keeps old errors and stale authority; C loses continuity. Session resumption and business authorization are different layers.

Follow the changed path:

case checkpoint → restored task/state → refreshed evidence and authority → next permitted step

The arrows show information or control moving between components; they do not imply that the model executes backend code itself.

Replay and inspect: Change the amount or expire approval between sessions. The restored case must request the necessary fresh check instead of submitting based on the old summary.

Changed-constraint decision — choose ONE before revealing: Only a small repository area matters. A: read every file into context; B: retain the map and search/read relevant paths as needed.

Reveal changed-constraint reasoning

B. Keep the route to evidence and fetch what matters. Context size alone does not guarantee relevant understanding.

Stage 2 — One source fails, but the report sounds complete

A legal research coordinator has two successful sources and one inaccessible mandatory source. The report combines the available facts and announces that every requirement is satisfied.

Original exam-style practice — choose ONE. Which synthesis and escalation pattern best preserves provenance and handles a failed mandatory dependency?

A. Hide the failed source because most work succeeded.

B. Discard all successful evidence and restart every worker.

C. Retain attributed successful findings, mark the failed dependency and withhold conclusions that require it; hand off the unresolved question and attempted recovery.

What is this asking? (optional, after reading the choices)

Identify the requirement that the current application fails to meet. Compare the proposed mechanisms against that requirement. Explain unfamiliar terms using the context above, then record a choice and your reason before opening the repair.

Reveal the repair and replay evidence

Choice C. Keep what is known, show what is not, and connect each conclusion to its evidence. A presents unsupported certainty; B wastes valid work. A useful handoff says what remains blocked and what would resolve it.

Follow the changed path:

successful sources → attributed claims
mandatory source failure → blocked dependent conclusion → actionable review packet

The arrows show information or control moving between components; they do not imply that the model executes backend code itself.

Replay and inspect: Inspect each final claim for source, date and uncertainty. A partial result must never be relabeled complete simply because its JSON fields are present.

Changed-constraint decision — choose ONE before revealing: Two credible sources disagree. A: silently average their claims; B: show both claims with scope/date and investigate or escalate the unresolved contradiction.

Reveal changed-constraint reasoning

B. Preserve the disagreement until evidence supports resolving it. Agreement cannot be manufactured by smoothing the prose.

Stage 3 — A strong average hides the difficult cases

An extraction system scores 96% on a sample dominated by utility bills. The team wants to remove human review of insurance claims too. Claims are rare in the sample and their high-risk fields have not been examined separately.

Original exam-style practice — choose ONE. Which evaluation best supports risk-based human review across heterogeneous document types?

A. Use the aggregate accuracy as proof every document type is ready.

B. Measure field/type-specific performance on representative labeled data, calibrate confidence and retain risk-based review plus sampled audits of high-confidence outputs.

C. Review only outputs where the model says it feels unsure, without calibration.

What is this asking? (optional, after reading the choices)

Identify the requirement that the current application fails to meet. Compare the proposed mechanisms against that requirement. Explain unfamiliar terms using the context above, then record a choice and your reason before opening the repair.

Reveal the repair and replay evidence

Choice B. Ask whether the score means the same thing for claim amounts as for utility invoice dates. A hides weak slices; C trusts a confidence number without checking what it predicts. Review capacity should follow error risk and demonstrated performance.

Follow the changed path:

labeled examples by type/field → measured errors and confidence calibration
contradiction / risk / uncertain field → human review; high confidence → sampled audit

The arrows show information or control moving between components; they do not imply that the model executes backend code itself.

Replay and inspect: Report the denominators and error rates for claims, bills and high-risk fields. Inspect confident mistakes as well as uncertain ones.

Changed-constraint decision — choose ONE before revealing: The reviewer can inspect only 10% of records. A: choose the first tenth; B: prioritize uncertain/contradictory/high-risk cases and reserve a stratified sample to detect confident failures.

Reveal changed-constraint reasoning

B. The review budget must detect both known risk and hidden overconfidence. No fixed threshold is justified solely by its numeric appearance.

5.0 The big idea

Long conversations and big tool outputs blur the details that matter, so keep key facts in a structured, source-linked state that is deliberately preserved and refreshed across summaries. Escalate for clear reasons (the customer asks, policy has a gap, no progress), not because someone sounds upset. Report failures and uncertainty honestly, with sources, instead of hiding them.

5.1 Keeping the facts that matter in long conversations

Plain ↔ technical · context window / compaction

Related objective example. Keep the current case's facts and chosen answer separate. For a decision, choose before opening this study help.

In everyday language

Keep a small working brief without losing the exact facts that decide the next action. A summary is useful only if critical uncertainty survives.

Does not establish: A compacted conversation is not a refreshed policy or authorization record, and shortening text must not erase an unresolved payment.

In technical terms

context window compaction durable state decision-critical facts source pointers

Inspect the technical example

Application checkpoint/brief pseudocode — fields are authored business state

durable_case = {
  case_id:"C-41", requested_amount_minor:18720,
  approval:{amount_minor:6000, policy:"v3"},
  current_policy:"v4", operation:{id:"OP-6", status:"unknown"},
  sources:["booking:B-52", "approval:A-6"]
}
brief = select_next_decision_facts(durable_case)

Read it as: The brief retains exact amounts, approval scope and unknown effects while source pointers permit inspection. Compaction manages model context; the application owns durable business facts.

Official mechanism reference

  • Progressive summarization blurs the details: amounts, percentages, dates and what the customer said they expected.
  • Position effects: important facts can be missed in long inputs, including material in the middle; no position guarantees reliable interpretation. Put a summary of key findings first and use clear section headers.
  • Tool results pile up. A 40-field order lookup where 5 fields matter wastes tokens. Trim tool output to the relevant fields before it builds up.
  • Keep a persistent "case facts" block (amounts, dates, order numbers, statuses) in every prompt, outside the summarized history. For sessions with several issues, keep a separate structured layer for each issue.
  • Upstream agents should return structured key facts and citations, not long reasoning, when downstream context is tight. Subagents include metadata such as dates and sources.
  • The saved guide asks for conversation continuity. In current application design, supply coherent relevant history; retain linked tool calls/results when needed and use verified summaries, exact case facts and retrieval for long work. Do not resend every raw message forever. A summary must preserve constraints, provenance and unresolved issues, not merely shorten prose.

5.2 Escalation and ambiguity

Related technical reference · context window / compaction

Escalate when:

  • the customer asks for a human. Do it right away, without investigating first.
  • policy has an exception or gap, or is silent. For example, a competitor price match when the policy only covers the company's own site.
  • the agent can't make meaningful progress.

Don't:

  • escalate based on sentiment or self-reported confidence. Neither reflects how complex the case really is.
  • escalate just because a case is complex.

Frustrated but the case is simple: acknowledge the frustration and offer to resolve it. Escalate if they ask again.

Watch out: low confidence is a bad escalation trigger but a valid review-routing signal for extractions, once the scores are calibrated (5.5). And conflicting sources get annotated with their attribution, not settled by whichever has the higher confidence score (5.6) (exam guide tasks 5.2, 5.5, 5.6, Tier 1).

Several matching customers: ask for another identifier. Don't guess using a heuristic.

Put the escalation criteria and few-shot examples in the system prompt.

5.3 Passing errors between agents

Related technical reference · context window / compaction

  • Pass up structured error context: failure type, what was tried, partial results, and alternatives.
  • An access failure (timeout) is not an empty result (no matches).
  • Both of these are anti-patterns:
    • silently suppressing errors by returning empty as if it succeeded
    • stopping the whole workflow because one part failed
  • Subagents recover locally from transient errors. The synthesis output carries coverage notes: which findings are well supported and where there are gaps.

5.4 Exploring a large codebase

Related technical reference · context window / compaction

  • Signs of degradation: inconsistent answers, and talk of "typical patterns" instead of the specific classes found earlier.
  • Scratchpad files keep key findings across context boundaries.
  • Subagents handle verbose exploration ("find all test files", "trace refund flow") while the main agent coordinates.
  • Summarize each phase before starting subagents for the next one.
  • Crash recovery: each agent exports its state to a known location, and the coordinator loads a manifest (a small index file listing each agent's saved state and where it is) when it resumes.
  • /compact frees space when context fills with verbose output. Compaction summarizes, so it can drop detail. Keep facts that must survive in a case-facts block or a file. /clear starts fresh; /context shows what is filling the window. (context guidance, checked 2026-10-21.)
  • Long-context design choices: load data just in time instead of upfront; server-side compaction; prompt caching, which cuts cost and latency but does not free context space; and the memory tool, which stores facts outside the window. A fifth lever is context editing, which clears old tool results or old thinking blocks server-side (beta header context-management-2025-06-27); the docs say compaction is generally preferred for long runs. (Source: https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/context-editing, checked 2026-10-21.)

These API features depend on supported models, platforms and configuration. They are current implementation context, not additional official objectives. Memory requires actual storage and retrieval; preserve exact decisions before clearing their supporting context.

5.5 Human review and confidence

Related technical reference · context window / compaction

  • 97% overall accuracy can hide poor performance on one document type or field. Check accuracy by document type and by field before you automate.
  • Use stratified random sampling (pick a random sample from each group, such as each document type, so small groups aren't missed) of high-confidence extractions to track error rates and spot new patterns.
  • Output confidence for each field and calibrate the thresholds on a labeled validation set (set the "send to a human" cut-off by checking which confidence scores were actually right on documents where you already know the answer). Send low-confidence or contradictory items to humans, whose time is limited.

5.6 Provenance and uncertainty

  • Summarizing loses attribution unless you keep claim-source mappings (URL, document, excerpt) all the way through synthesis.
  • Conflicting statistics from credible sources: note the conflict and credit each source. Don't pick one at random. Let the coordinator decide how to reconcile them.
  • Record dates (publication or collection) so a change over time isn't mistaken for a contradiction.
  • Separate what's well established from what's contested. Show financial data as tables, news as prose, and technical findings as lists.

Worked example

Related technical reference · context window / compaction

After 30 turns, the support agent's summary says "the customer wants a refund" and has lost the $247.83 amount and the order number. Answer: a persistent case-facts block kept outside the summarized history, with explicit preservation and refresh checks. In this case the defect is lossy state management; a larger context window alone does not repair the missing facts. Larger capacity can still help when measured limits remain after state and retrieval design are sound.

Traps

  • "Escalate when the customer sounds angry."
  • "Pick the most recent customer record when there are 3 matches."
  • "Return an empty list when the search tool times out."
  • "97% accuracy, so remove human review."

Try it (45 min)

Take (or write) a 30-turn support transcript that mentions a dollar amount and an order number early on. Write a case-facts block for it: customer ID, order number, amount, dates, status, and what the customer expects. Then have Claude summarize the transcript with the block kept outside the summary, and compare.

Done when the dollar amount and the order number survive the summary word for word.
